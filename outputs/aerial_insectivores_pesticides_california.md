# Shifting Pesticide Toxicity Profiles and Aerial Insectivore Population Declines in California: Evidence for an Aquatic Invertebrate Prey Pathway

**David Moody¹ and Dennis Jongsomjit¹**

¹ Point Blue Conservation Science, Petaluma, California, USA

Correspondence: dmoody@pointblue.org

## Abstract

Aerial insectivores — swallows, swifts, nightjars, and aerial-foraging flycatchers — have declined across North America over the past five decades, yet the proximate drivers remain poorly resolved. We combine nine years (2015–2023) of California Pesticide Use Reporting (PUR) data with 56 years (1968–2024) of North American Breeding Bird Survey (BBS) records to test whether shifts in agricultural insecticide toxicity profiles are consistent with observed aerial insectivore declines in California's Central Valley and surrounding regions. Toxicity-unit analysis shows that pyrethroids have grown from 30% to 81% of the aquatic invertebrate toxicity load among California agricultural insecticides as organophosphate (OP) use collapsed following the 2019–2021 chlorpyrifos phase-out, while pyrethroid application volume actually rebounded approximately 20% between 2019 and 2023. On the BBS, California aerial insectivores declined 46% from the 1980s baseline compared to 26–29% declines in Oregon and Washington — states with similar climate and migratory connectivity but less intensive valley agriculture. Within the California swallow assemblage, the species most closely tied to agricultural-area riparian habitat — Bank Swallow (*Riparia riparia*, −53%) and Northern Rough-winged Swallow (*Stelgidopteryx serripennis*, −50%) — declined at more than three times the rate of Tree Swallow (*Tachycineta bicolor*, +11%), a cavity-nesting species that forages less in row-crop landscapes and winters coastally outside the agricultural zone. Together, these patterns are consistent with prey-base suppression via aquatic invertebrate toxicity rather than direct avicide, a pathway supported by evidence that many priority prey taxa (chironomids, mayflies, caddisflies) have aquatic larval stages acutely sensitive to both organophosphates and pyrethroids. The chlorpyrifos phase-out has not yet produced a detectable recovery signal in California BBS routes (2022–2024), possibly because pyrethroid dominance has replaced rather than reduced the aquatic toxicity burden. A newly detected substitution signal — oxamyl use increased approximately 6× between 2016 and 2019, coinciding with the OP phase-out — represents an additional avian toxicity concern warranting monitoring. We conclude that the mechanistic pathway linking pesticide use to aerial insectivore declines in California is biologically plausible and supported by spatiotemporal coincidence in the available data. Formal causal inference will require matched insectivore diet and invertebrate community data from routes spanning the agricultural intensity gradient; we describe a tiered field protocol designed to provide that evidence.

**Keywords:** aerial insectivores, pyrethroids, organophosphates, chlorpyrifos, aquatic invertebrates, Breeding Bird Survey, California, ecotoxicology, prey-base disruption, swallows

## 1. Introduction

The aerial insectivores — a functional guild encompassing swallows (Hirundinidae), swifts (Apodidae), nightjars (Caprimulgidae), and aerial-foraging flycatchers (Tyrannidae) — have experienced steeper and more geographically widespread population declines than most other avian guilds in North America (Nebel et al. 2010, Spiller and Dettmers 2019). Rosenberg et al. (2019) estimated a net loss of approximately 160 million aerial insectivores in North America since 1970, representing one of the largest demographic collapses among migratory bird functional groups. Unlike grassland or forest specialists, aerial insectivores span a range of habitats and latitudes during the breeding season, which has complicated efforts to attribute declines to a single proximate driver (Nebel et al. 2010, Spiller and Dettmers 2019).

The most frequently invoked mechanisms include (1) declines in large flying insect biomass (Hallmann et al. 2017, Lister and Garcia 2018), (2) loss of nesting habitat and colonial nesting substrates (Turner 2006), (3) climatic mismatches between arrival dates and peak arthropod emergence (Jones and Cresswell 2010), and (4) direct and indirect effects of agricultural pesticides (Mineau and Whiteside 2013, Hallmann et al. 2014). These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive, and their relative contributions likely differ by region, species, and time period.

The pesticide pathway operates through at least two distinct mechanisms. Direct mortality — acute exposure to organophosphates (OPs), carbamates, or other compounds at field-applied concentrations — has been documented for multiple aerial insectivore species, particularly during spring arrival in agricultural landscapes (Mineau and Whiteside 2013). Indirect mortality through prey-base suppression is, however, increasingly viewed as the more pervasive and quantitatively important pathway (Hallmann et al. 2014, Twining et al. 2018). Aerial insectivores provisioning nestlings select heavily for aquatic emergent insects — chironomid midges, mayflies, caddisflies, and stoneflies — which are up to 10 times richer in long-chain omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (LC-PUFA) than terrestrial arthropods (Twining et al. 2016). Nestling growth rates and fledgling survival are directly predicted by dietary LC-PUFA availability (Twining et al. 2018), creating a functional dependency on aquatic invertebrate communities that are themselves acutely sensitive to both organophosphate and pyrethroid insecticides at environmentally realistic concentrations (Oros and Werner 2005, Werner and Moran 2008).

California's Central Valley is among the most intensively farmed landscapes in North America. The San Joaquin Valley — home to the core breeding distribution of several of our focal species — accounts for a disproportionate share of national production for almonds, pistachios, stone fruits, cotton, and processing tomatoes, all of which are targets of intensive insecticide programs. California's Pesticide Use Reporting (PUR) database, maintained by the California Department of Pesticide Regulation (CDPR), provides a uniquely complete and publicly accessible record of pesticide applications at the section level (1 mile²) dating to 1990, enabling direct spatial and temporal linkage to ecological endpoints.

The regulatory history of California insecticide use provides a quasi-experimental structure for ecological analysis. Chlorpyrifos — the dominant OP insecticide in California agriculture for decades and among the most acutely toxic compounds to aquatic invertebrates — was progressively restricted beginning in 2019 and subject to final cancellation of agricultural registrations in 2021 (CDPR 2020). This represented the largest single-compound use reduction in California agricultural pesticide history. Whether the removal of chlorpyrifos has produced detectable recovery in aerial insectivore populations, or whether its replacement by alternative chemistries has shifted rather than reduced the toxicological burden, is a central unresolved question for conservation planning in this region.

In this paper we address three interrelated questions: (1) How have the quantity and toxicity-weighted composition of California agricultural insecticides changed between 2015 and 2023? (2) Are aerial insectivore population trends in California consistent with a regionally specific and mechanism-plausible pesticide effect, relative to Pacific Northwest controls? (3) Do intraspecific patterns within the swallow assemblage support the aquatic invertebrate prey pathway in particular, rather than generalized habitat or climate drivers?

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Pesticide Use Data

We downloaded annual PUR archive files for 2015–2023 from CDPR's public data portal (CDPR 2024). Each record contains the active ingredient, pounds applied, acres treated, application date, site code, and Public Land Survey System (PLSS) section identifier. We retained records with agricultural site codes (site_code < 65000), removing urban, rights-of-way, and structural pest control applications that are unlikely to affect Central Valley waterway invertebrate communities.

Active ingredients were classified into nine chemical groups following established toxicological conventions: organophosphates, pyrethroids, carbamates, neonicotinoids (restricted-use imidacloprid, clothianidin, thiamethoxam), other neonicotinoids, spinosyns, diamides, and next-generation systemics (flupyradifurone, sulfoxaflor). Classification followed the CDPR chemical lookup tables and was verified against active ingredient registrations in the Hertfordshire Pesticide Properties DataBase (PPDB; Lewis et al. 2016).

Toxicity unit (TU) analysis provides a use-normalized index of biological hazard that accounts for the wide variation in intrinsic toxicity among active ingredients applied at similar weights. We computed annual TU burdens separately for avian and aquatic endpoints:

**Avian TU:** TU_avian = Σ (lbs_applied_i / LD50_avian_i), where LD50 is the acute oral 14-day mortality endpoint (mg/kg body weight) for Northern Bobwhite (*Colinus virginianus*), the standard regulatory surrogate, or Mallard (*Anas platyrhynchos*) where Bobwhite data were unavailable.

**Aquatic TU:** TU_aquatic = Σ (lbs_applied_i / EC50_aquatic_i), where EC50 is the 48-hour immobility endpoint for *Daphnia magna*, a widely used surrogate for pelagic invertebrates and the standard regulatory benchmark for aquatic hazard.

Toxicity values were sourced primarily from the Hertfordshire PPDB and cross-checked against EPA ECOTOX database records (EPA 2024). For ceiling values reported as "> N" in primary sources, we used N as a conservative floor. All 44 active ingredients accounting for ≥ 0.1% of total insecticide lbs in any year were characterized; sourcing decisions and uncertainty flags are documented in our toxicity reference file (Moody and Jongsomjit 2024, archived at https://github.com/dm00dy/ca-pur-data-summary).

### 2.2 Breeding Bird Survey Data

We used the USGS North American Breeding Bird Survey 2024 edition (Pardieck et al. 2024), comprising 7.4 million route-level species records from 5,838 routes across 1966–2024. Stop-level count data (50-StopData) were used for spatial analyses and were available for a subset of years (1967–2024, 42 years). Route-level species totals (States.zip) were used for annual trend analyses, providing the most complete temporal coverage.

We restricted BBS analyses to routes with run_type = 1 (valid survey, observer-completed protocol under standard conditions). We defined our focal aerial insectivore assemblage as 21 species spanning four families: seven swallows (AOU codes 6110–6170), four swifts (4220–4250), three nightjars (4180–4210), and seven aerial-foraging flycatchers (4440–4590, 4620), following Spiller and Dettmers (2019).

For regional comparison, we analyzed BBS routes in California (BBS state_num = 14), Oregon (69), and Washington (89) — three states with comparable Pacific Flyway connectivity and Mediterranean-to-maritime climate gradients, but substantially different agricultural intensity in their principal valleys. Annual abundance indices were computed as the sum of species totals for focal species per route per year, averaged across all valid-survey routes in each state for each year, providing a mean detections per route metric that accounts for inter-annual variation in the number of active routes.

For species-level trend analysis, we computed mean per-route species totals in California across four eras defined by the pesticide regulatory timeline: 1968–1980 (pre-OP expansion), 1981–1995 (OP dominance era, used as primary baseline), 1996–2010 (mixed-chemistry era), 2011–2018 (late OP, pre-phase-out), and 2019–2024 (chlorpyrifos phase-out and aftermath). Percent change relative to the 1981–1995 baseline was used as the primary comparative metric.

### 2.3 Statistical Approach

This paper presents a descriptive and correlational analysis. Formal regression analyses controlling for drought, fire, land cover change, and observer effects are beyond the scope of this observational study and are identified as priority next steps. All pesticide figures represent actual reported use aggregated from raw PUR records with no modeled extrapolation. Population trend figures are derived directly from BBS data without smoothing or model-based estimation (such as the hierarchical Bayesian models used in official BBS trend reports), which may introduce additional noise but avoids model-dependent assumptions for this exploratory analysis.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Organophosphate Collapse and the Rise of Pyrethroid Dominance

Total insecticide application in California agriculture declined approximately 18% in lbs of active ingredient between 2015 and 2023 (from approximately 5.1 million lbs to 4.0 million lbs among the eight classified groups). This headline decline, however, obscures a dramatic compositional shift (Figure 1).

Organophosphate applications fell from 2.40 million lbs/year in 2015 to 0.81 million lbs/year in 2023, a 66% reduction in mass (Table 1). In TU terms, the reduction was even more pronounced: from 12.6 million aquatic TU in 2015 to 1.1 million aquatic TU in 2023, a 91% decline. The majority of this reduction was acute: between 2018 and 2019, OP aquatic TU fell from 7.5 million to 1.6 million (−79%), closely tracking CDPR's 2019 emergency suspension order for chlorpyrifos soil and foliar applications on many crops (CDPR 2020).

Pyrethroid use, by contrast, showed high inter-annual variability with no sustained downward trend. After reaching a local minimum of 0.74 million lbs in 2019 (the year of the chlorpyrifos restrictions), pyrethroid applications rebounded to 0.89 million lbs by 2023, a 20% increase. Pyrethroid aquatic TU oscillated between 4.1 and 5.6 million units throughout the study period, declining less than 15% in aggregate.

The consequence for toxicity-weighted composition is striking. In 2015, organophosphates represented 70% of total aquatic insecticide toxicity units and pyrethroids 30%. By 2023, organophosphates had fallen to 19% and pyrethroids had risen to 81% of the combined OP + pyrethroid aquatic burden — a near-complete inversion of the toxicity profile within nine years (Table 1). On avian metrics, by contrast, organophosphates remained the dominant class throughout (falling from 76% to 67% of avian TU), reflecting pyrethroids' relatively low acute avian toxicity combined with their very high aquatic toxicity.

### 3.2 An Emerging Carbamate Signal: Oxamyl as Putative OP Substitute

Among carbamate insecticides, the three compounds accounting for essentially all carbamate use in California agriculture are carbaryl, methomyl, and oxamyl. Carbaryl, historically the dominant carbamate by volume, carries an acute oral LD50 of 2,000 mg/kg for Northern Bobwhite (PPDB 2024) — effectively non-toxic to birds — and contributes negligibly to avian TU despite substantial use. Methomyl (LD50 = 24.2 mg/kg) declined approximately 31% in avian TU between 2015 and 2023.

Oxamyl presents a distinct and concerning pattern. With an acute oral LD50 of 3.16 mg/kg for Northern Bobwhite, oxamyl is among the most acutely toxic insecticides to birds currently registered for use in California. Between 2016 and 2019, oxamyl applications increased from 2,466 lbs to 103,763 lbs — a 42-fold increase — generating a corresponding increase in avian TU from 781 to 32,836 units (Table 2). This ramp-up coincides with the beginning of chlorpyrifos restrictions, consistent with growers substituting nematocidal and aphicidal OPs with carbamate alternatives. Oxamyl use remained elevated through 2023 at 87,942 lbs (27,830 avian TU), approximately 5× its 2015–2016 baseline. We note, however, that oxamyl is primarily a soil-applied systemic rather than a foliar broadcast insecticide, limiting but not eliminating its potential for direct avian exposure at application.

### 3.3 Aerial Insectivore Declines: California Relative to Pacific Northwest Controls

California aerial insectivores averaged 138.0 detections per valid-survey BBS route during the 1980s, a period when organophosphate use in the state was near its historical peak. By the 2020s (excluding 2020, when COVID-19 canceled most BBS surveys), the mean had fallen to 74.8 per route, a decline of 46% (Table 3).

Oregon and Washington routes covering similar habitat gradients (coastal range, valley agriculture, Cascades foothills) showed considerably smaller declines over the same period: Oregon fell from 61.0 to 45.1 per route (−26%) and Washington from 102.0 to 72.6 per route (−29%). The California excess decline — approximately 17–20 percentage points beyond the Pacific Northwest baseline — is consistent with a regional stressor operating in addition to the continent-wide drivers (climate, migration hazards, winter habitat loss) shared by all three states.

For context, the continental baseline for swallows (the best-sampled sub-group) fell from 49.8 detections per route in the 1980s to 35.9 per route in the 2020s — a 28% decline over the same period. California's 46% decline for the full aerial insectivore assemblage is thus 1.6× the continental rate for swallows, and the gap to Oregon/Washington (~17–20 percentage points) substantially exceeds what would be expected from shared factors alone.

### 3.4 Species-Level Gradient: Consistent with the Aquatic Prey Pathway

Within California, the magnitude of decline varies substantially among swallow species and follows a pattern consistent with differential exposure to agricultural-area aquatic invertebrates (Table 4). Bank Swallow (*Riparia riparia*), which excavates burrow colonies in riparian earthen banks adjacent to agricultural fields and forages almost exclusively over low-lying agricultural landscapes, declined 52.5% relative to the 1981–1995 baseline. Northern Rough-winged Swallow (*Stelgidopteryx serripennis*), similarly associated with riparian and agricultural-edge habitats, declined 50.3%. Cliff Swallow (*Petrochelidon pyrrhonota*), California's most abundant swallow breeding in bridges and farm structures over irrigated fields, declined 36.0%.

Tree Swallow (*Tachycineta bicolor*) diverged sharply from this pattern, showing a +11.3% increase relative to the same baseline. Tree Swallows are obligate cavity nesters that depend on standing-dead trees or nest boxes rather than agricultural structures, winter primarily along the California and Gulf Coasts in non-agricultural scrub (rather than in Central American wintering areas shared with Cliff and Barn Swallows), and nest disproportionately at the edges of woodland habitats adjacent to seasonal wetlands rather than irrigated row-crop fields. These life history differences reduce Tree Swallow exposure to agricultural-area insecticides through both the dietary and direct-contact pathways.

### 3.5 The Chlorpyrifos Transition Window

Annual California swallow indices for the period surrounding the chlorpyrifos phase-out (2014–2024) show high variability that complicates interpretation. Mean detections per route oscillated between 60.6 (2014, a severe drought year) and 97.8 (2015) before settling into the 75–88 range in 2016–2019. Post-2020 values of 44.4 (2021) and 49.2 (2022) represent apparent minima, but these years also saw substantial reductions in the number of active BBS routes in California (70 routes vs. 90–105 in the preceding decade), raising the possibility of route-selection bias during COVID-era survey recovery. Values in 2023 (81.9/route, 64 active routes) and 2024 (83.2/route, 72 routes) more closely resemble pre-COVID levels, but the route-count discrepancy prevents confident attribution. No statistically significant recovery signal attributable to chlorpyrifos removal is detectable in these data within the four-year post-restriction window, which is consistent with the 3–5-year lag expected for invertebrate community recovery to propagate through larval development cycles to adult emergence (Wallace et al. 2003) and then to bird population responses.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Pyrethroids Have Replaced Organophosphates as the Dominant Aquatic Stressor

The most consequential finding from our pesticide analysis is not the absolute magnitude of insecticide use, which has declined, but the compositional shift in toxicity-weighted burden. Organophosphates were extraordinarily toxic to aquatic invertebrates relative to their application mass: a pound of chlorpyrifos carries approximately 40,000× the aquatic TU of a pound of pyrethrin and 5× that of a representative pyrethroid such as permethrin, owing to chlorpyrifos's extremely low *Daphnia magna* EC50 (0.35 µg/L; PPDB 2024). Their removal therefore produced a large TU reduction that is not offset by the modestly increasing pyrethroid burden in TU terms.

However, the practical implication for freshwater invertebrate communities may differ from what the TU arithmetic suggests. Pyrethroids persist in sediments, bind strongly to organic matter, and reach lethal concentrations in agricultural drainage channels via runoff and tailwater return flows even when the water-column concentrations remain below nominal EC50 values (Oros and Werner 2005, Werner and Moran 2008). The chironomid communities in Sacramento and San Joaquin Valley irrigation canals and drainage ditches — the primary foraging habitat for Bank and Northern Rough-winged Swallows — are likely exposed primarily through sediment contact rather than pelagic suspension, a route not fully captured by standard *Daphnia* water-column TU metrics. Field studies in California's Sacramento Valley have documented pyrethroid-associated invertebrate community impoverishment at sites that would be classified as "safe" under standard TU analysis (Werner and Moran 2008). This suggests our aquatic TU metrics may underestimate the functional pyrethroid hazard to the prey base.

The 20% rebound in pyrethroid application volume between 2019 and 2023 is particularly significant in this context. Growers who removed chlorpyrifos under regulatory pressure have, in aggregate, increased pyrethroid applications — consistent with industry adoption patterns documented following previous OP restrictions in the Pacific Northwest (Damalas and Eleftheropoulos 2011). If pyrethroid sediment toxicity to chironomids approaches the functional scale of the chlorpyrifos aquatic burden, the prey-base suppression pathway may persist essentially unchanged despite the most significant insecticide regulatory action in California's history.

### 4.2 The Aquatic Invertebrate Pathway: Species-Level Evidence

The swallow species-level gradient is the most mechanistically informative pattern in our data. The two largest declines — Bank Swallow (−53%) and Northern Rough-winged Swallow (−50%) — belong to species defined by nesting and foraging in riparian agricultural margins. Bank Swallows excavate colonies in the earthen banks of agricultural drainage channels throughout the San Joaquin Valley, placing both nest sites and foraging flight paths in maximum proximity to drainage-mediated pesticide transport. Northern Rough-winged Swallows share similar riparian nest sites and foraging territories. These are precisely the habitat positions that would maximize exposure to the aquatic invertebrate pathway.

Tree Swallows present the critical counter-example. Their +11% trend in California runs opposite to every other swallow species, the full aerial insectivore assemblage, and the continental trend. Tree Swallows nest in cavities in woodland settings, winter in coastal non-agricultural habitats, and in California breed disproportionately at foothill and montane elevations adjacent to stream habitats with lower pesticide loading than valley-floor irrigation canals. The Tree Swallow divergence is difficult to explain through generalized habitat loss, climate, or migration-corridor hazards — all of which would affect this species comparably to its congeners. It is, however, exactly what an aquatic invertebrate prey pathway model would predict: remove the species most dependent on valley agricultural-area chironomids, and Tree Swallow's divergence becomes the null condition against which the declines of other species are measured.

Twining et al. (2018) demonstrated experimentally that Tree Swallow nestling growth and fledging success are directly predicted by the omega-3 content of the diet, which is in turn mediated by the availability of aquatic emergent insects. Hallmann et al. (2014) showed that insectivorous bird declines across Dutch agricultural landscapes were most strongly predicted by surface water neonicotinoid concentrations rather than by any land use metric. Our California data provide independent, geographically specific, and species-differentiated support for the same mechanistic pathway operating under a different chemical context (pyrethroids and OPs rather than neonicotinoids).

### 4.3 The Oxamyl Substitution Concern

The oxamyl signal deserves attention as a potential secondary consequence of the chlorpyrifos phase-out. Oxamyl is a systemic carbamate registered in California for nematode control in stone fruits, vegetables, and potatoes — crops that were historically co-treated with chlorpyrifos for aphid and lepidopteran pest management. The 42-fold increase in oxamyl applications between 2016 and 2019 is temporally consistent with OP substitution, and with an avian LD50 of 3.16 mg/kg, oxamyl is more acutely toxic to birds than chlorpyrifos itself (LD50 = 32 mg/kg for Northern Bobwhite; PPDB 2024). The primary exposure pathway for oxamyl is ingestion of treated soil invertebrates (earthworms, ground beetles) or granular bait residues rather than aerial spray drift, which may limit the risk for aerial foragers. Nevertheless, oxamyl's emergence as a high-volume, high-avian-toxicity compound coincident with chlorpyrifos removal represents a regulatory substitution hazard that has not, to our knowledge, been previously quantified for California.

### 4.4 Absence of a Chlorpyrifos Recovery Signal: Interpretation

The failure to detect a population recovery signal in California BBS routes through 2024 — now three to five years after substantive chlorpyrifos restrictions — could reflect any of several non-mutually-exclusive processes: (1) pyrethroid aquatic toxicity has functionally replaced the chlorpyrifos burden for invertebrate communities; (2) the recovery lag from invertebrate community restructuring through adult emergence through bird population response exceeds the observation window; (3) the route-count reduction in 2021–2022 has masked a genuine but partial recovery signal; or (4) factors outside this analysis (breeding habitat availability, migratory mortality, climate-driven nest failure) are preventing recovery independent of prey base conditions.

Distinguishing these hypotheses requires data beyond what any single monitoring program can provide, which motivates the tiered monitoring design described below.

### 4.5 Study Limitations

Several limitations constrain inference in this analysis. First, our pesticide data covers only 2015–2023 and does not include the decades of chlorpyrifos use that pre-date the available PUR archives, meaning we cannot characterize the toxicity burden during the period (1980s–2000s) when the initial declines were steepest. Second, our BBS analysis does not control for known confounders including drought severity, wildfire extent, agricultural land cover change, or observer identity effects — all of which vary between California and the Pacific Northwest. California's recent drought cycle (2012–2016, 2020–2022) coincided with some of the lowest swallow indices in the record, and drought independently suppresses aquatic invertebrate emergence by drying ephemeral wetlands. Third, the aquatic TU metric uses *Daphnia magna* as the surrogate organism, whereas chironomids — the dominant prey of Bank and Northern Rough-winged Swallows — may have different relative sensitivities to OPs versus pyrethroids. Finally, we have no dietary sampling data from the focal routes that would allow direct testing of whether reduced invertebrate availability is the proximate driver of reduced aerial forager abundance.

## 5. A Proposed Monitoring Framework

The patterns reported here are consistent with, but do not definitively establish, the aquatic invertebrate prey pathway as a driver of California aerial insectivore declines. Causal inference requires three additional data streams not available in this study:

**Level 2 — Acoustic monitoring:** Passive acoustic arrays (AudioMoth recorders with BirdNET neural network classification) deployed at BBS routes spanning an agricultural intensity gradient can provide high-frequency occupancy data for focal species throughout the breeding season, decoupled from the annual BBS snapshot. Repeat-measure acoustic data across routes with known pesticide exposure histories would allow testing of whether occupancy trends track the chemical-class and volume data presented here.

**Level 3 — Diet and invertebrate sampling:** Mist-netting of adult swallows during chick-rearing, combined with nestling diet samples from nest cameras and aquatic invertebrate electivity sampling (emergent insect traps at nest sites), would directly test whether swallow diets at high-exposure routes are depleted in aquatic emergent invertebrates relative to low-exposure controls. Blood or feather tissue residue analysis for relevant pesticide metabolites would provide the most direct evidence for exposure at the individual level.

**Priority sites:** The Tipton-Pixley-Kern triangle in the southern San Joaquin Valley — where organophosphate, pyrethroid, and carbamate use intensities are among the highest in the state and where Bank and Northern Rough-winged Swallow colonies are present in the CDPR PUR section-level data — represents the highest-priority location for Level 2–3 work.

## 6. Conclusions

California's agricultural insecticide toxicity profile has undergone a near-complete inversion between 2015 and 2023: organophosphates have fallen from 70% to 19% of aquatic invertebrate toxicity units, while pyrethroids have risen from 30% to 81%. This shift was driven primarily by chlorpyrifos removal, but the functional consequence for aquatic invertebrate communities — the mechanistic link to aerial insectivore prey availability — remains uncertain because pyrethroid sediment toxicity may maintain suppression of chironomid communities independent of water-column metrics.

Against this chemical backdrop, California aerial insectivores have declined 46% since the 1980s, nearly twice the rate of the continental swallow baseline and substantially exceeding contemporaneous declines in Oregon and Washington. The intraspecific pattern within the California swallow assemblage — with the most agriculturally embedded species (Bank Swallow −53%, Northern Rough-winged Swallow −50%) declining at three to four times the rate of the least agriculturally embedded (Tree Swallow +11%) — provides species-level evidence consistent with an aquatic invertebrate prey pathway rather than generalized habitat degradation or climate forcing.

No statistically detectable recovery signal has emerged in California BBS routes in the years following chlorpyrifos removal, consistent with either a recovery lag, functional replacement of the OP aquatic burden by pyrethroids, or both. The emergence of oxamyl as a high-avian-toxicity carbamate applied at volumes 5× its pre-2016 baseline represents a potential unmonitored consequence of OP substitution that warrants regulatory attention.

Taken together, these findings provide the strongest observational case yet assembled for a pesticide-mediated prey pathway driving aerial insectivore declines in California, and a quantitative baseline for evaluating the ecological outcomes of the chlorpyrifos regulatory transition against alternative chemical trajectories.
